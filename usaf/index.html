A missile field the size of Vermont.

I was a nuclear weapons journeyman in the munitions squadron at F.E. Warren Air Force Base, Wyoming. Then I moved to Vermont, which is — to within sixteen square miles — the same size as the ground I used to work.

Cheyenne sits at the southeast corner of Wyoming, and three miles west of it is the oldest continuously active installation in the Air Force — founded in 1867 as a cavalry post, still open, now flying nothing at all. F.E. Warren has no runway. What it has is a field.

The field is a hundred and fifty holes in the ground spread across three states, and fifteen buried capsules where two officers sit a shift at a time. Between them is ranch country: interstate, then county road, then gravel, then a chain-link square in the middle of somebody's grazing land with a concrete lid on it. Wing personnel drive around seven and a half million miles a year to keep that arrangement working. When the snow closes the roads, helicopters take over.

The 90th's field covers 9,600 square miles. Vermont covers 9,616. I did not plan that. I found it out writing this page, and had to sit with it for a minute. 90th Missile Wing fact sheet · US Census

What a 2W2 actually does

The Air Force career field is 2W2X1 — nuclear weapons. The X is a placeholder for your skill level, and the ladder runs helper, apprentice, journeyman, craftsman, superintendent, which in the numbers is 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. Journeyman is the rung where you stop being supervised on the task and start being the one who certifies somebody else on it.

The published description of the specialty is dry to the point of comedy: inspect, maintain, store, handle, modify, repair, and account for nuclear weapons and their components. The training pipeline runs through the 363rd Training Squadron at Sheppard AFB in Texas before anyone touches anything real. What the words leave out is the texture of it — that the whole discipline is built out of two-person rules, written procedure, and a culture where the correct response to "that's probably fine" is to stop the job.

My squadron was the 90th Munitions Squadron, which the Air Force stood up in 2009 and renamed in 2011 when nuclear munitions moved under the command that operates them daily. Its public description is payloads and reentry systems: the Mk-12A and the Mk-21.

The part that stayed with me

The three warheads in that exhibit are not three versions of one idea. They are three different decades of thinking about what a weapon owes the people who handle it.

The W62 went into the field in 1970 and left it in 2010, and it was built before the safety engineering that the rest of the stockpile now takes for granted. The W87 that replaced it has insensitive high explosive — a filler you can shoot, drop, or set on fire without it detonating — and a fire-resistant pit rated to hold together in a thousand-degree fire for hours. The point of both is the same: a crash, a fire, or a fall should end in a mess and a very bad day, and not in a nuclear yield or a plutonium plume.

The W87s came off the Peacekeeper missiles when that system was retired, and were moved onto Minuteman III over the back half of the 2000s. One W87 replaced one W62, or three W78s. So the same program that shrank the force also swapped the oldest, least forgiving warhead in it for the newest and safest. Both things are true at once, which is roughly my whole experience of that job.

Why any of this shows up on a developer's site

Because it is where I learned the habits I still work by, and they transfer more directly than anyone expects.

  • Two-person integrity. Nothing consequential gets done by one person alone and unwatched. That is a code review, and I had it drilled into me a decade before I had a pull request.
  • Written procedure beats recall. You do not remember the steps. You read the steps, and you read them again on the step you have done four hundred times, because that is the one you will get wrong.
  • Stop the job. Anyone, any rank, any time, for any reason, at zero social cost. Most engineering cultures claim this. Very few of them mean it.
  • Accountability is a system, not a feeling. Every item is on a record somewhere, and the record and the physical world are reconciled on a schedule. That is an audit log with a nightly consistency check.

I am aware how it reads to put nuclear weapons on a portfolio site next to a robot who rides a motorcycle. But the AI systems I build now are the same shape of problem: they take actions, the actions have consequences, and the interesting engineering is almost entirely in what happens when something goes wrong. I got a very thorough education in that.

What's next for the field

Minuteman III is being replaced by the LGM-35A Sentinel, and F.E. Warren is first in line. The program has not gone smoothly: it breached its cost baseline by about eighty-one percent in 2024 and was restructured rather than cancelled. As of early 2026 the GAO expects the first flight test in 2028 and says Minuteman III may have to stay on alert until 2050 — eighty years for a missile whose production line closed in 1978.

Somebody is out there in the snow right now, driving a gravel road toward a chain-link square, with a checklist.
